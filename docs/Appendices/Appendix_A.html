17  Appendix A: Field Campaign, Data and Processing

17.1 Introduction and Context

This appendix describes the field monitoring campaign, data acquisition programmes and processing procedures undertaken to support the numerical modelling framework developed under Project B of the WAMSI Mardie Offset Marine and Intertidal Research Program. Project B aims to improve understanding of intertidal ecosystem dynamics along the West Pilbara coast, with a particular focus on mangroves and cyanobacterial mats and their responses to environmental change and coastal development.

Although the primary objective of Project B is numerical modelling, reliable field observations are essential for defining model boundary conditions, calibrating hydrodynamic parameters and evaluating model performance. The field data presented here were collected through a field campaign undertaken both independently as well as in collaboration with Projects A and C. The campaign combined hydrodynamic sensor deployments, topographic LiDAR surveys and high resolution bathymetric surveys of tidal creeks across the principal study areas.

17.2 Field Campaign Strategy and Evolution

The field campaign followed a phased implementation plan designed to address the logistical challenges associated with working in remote coastal environments of the Pilbara. An initial reconnaissance survey was conducted in June and July 2023 to assess site accessibility, identify potential instrument deployment locations and establish logistical requirements. This was followed by three operational phases.

During Phase 1, initial water level and environmental loggers were installed across the candidate study sites. The first maintenance period provided an opportunity to assess data quality, instrument accessibility and the hydrodynamic value of each monitoring location. Following this assessment, the monitoring programme was streamlined, with subsequent deployments and maintenance activities prioritising the Giralia and Urala systems. The main activities and operational milestones are summarised in Table 17.1.

17.3 Monitoring Network and Data Collection

To characterise the hydrodynamic gradients influencing mangrove and cyanobacterial mat distribution, instruments were deployed along cross shore transects extending from lower estuarine channels and mid creek reaches to upper tidal areas and the margins of cyanobacterial mats. Water level loggers recorded pressure and temperature, while selected instruments also recorded electrical conductivity for the estimation of salinity. Dedicated barometric pressure stations were installed at Giralia and Urala to measure atmospheric pressure fluctuations required for correcting the water pressure records.

The wider monitoring network, including baseline elevations and the distribution of groundwater monitoring locations, is presented in Figure 17.3. 17.4 Topographic and Bathymetric Surveys

Accurate elevation data are fundamental to the representation of intertidal topography and tidal conveyance in hydrodynamic and morphodynamic models. Regional elevation datasets generally lack the vertical resolution required to represent shallow intertidal channels and subtle topographic variations. A multi scale elevation data acquisition programme was therefore undertaken to improve the representation of the study areas.

High resolution aerial LiDAR surveys were acquired across the principal study sites to characterise the elevation of supratidal platforms, mangrove areas and surrounding intertidal surfaces. The LiDAR products provide detailed information on the elevation and topographic structure of the coastal landscape and form an important spatial dataset for the development of the hydrodynamic model domains.

The LiDAR derived elevation models for Giralia, Urala and Cape Preston are presented in Figure 17.5 - Figure 17.7.

17.4.1 Vegetation Structure and Canopy Height Extraction

Vegetation structure and canopy height were derived from airborne LiDAR point cloud data using automated Python processing workflows. The point clouds were classified into ground and non-ground returns, allowing vegetation heights to be estimated relative to the underlying ground surface. Height above ground (HAG) was calculated by referencing each vegetation point to the surrounding ground elevation. Points with heights below \(0.05\,\text{m}\) were excluded to minimise the influence of background noise and low lying returns from bare sediment and salt flats.

The remaining vegetation points were classified into three structural categories based on their height above ground: low vegetation (\(0.2 \leq HAG < 1.0\,\text{m}\)), medium vegetation (\(1.0 \leq HAG < 3.0\,\text{m}\)) and high vegetation (\(HAG \geq 3.0\,\text{m}\)). This classification provided a consistent representation of vegetation structure across Giralia, Urala and Cape Preston. The classified point cloud data were subsequently used to generate spatial representations of canopy height and vegetation distribution at a spatial resolution of \(1\,\text{m}\).

In addition to canopy height, vegetation point density was derived from the LiDAR data to characterise the spatial distribution and relative density of vegetation across the study areas. These structural metrics provide complementary information on canopy height and density, supporting the characterisation of mangrove structure and the development of spatial vegetation parameters for the hydrodynamic and ecological modelling framework.

17.5 Sediment Characteristics and Particle Size Distributions

Sediment samples collected from the Urala and Giralia mudflats represent contrasting substrate types, ranging from relatively sandy material associated with channels and exposed coastal surfaces to finer grained sediments characteristic of sheltered mudflats. These differences provide context for interpreting the sedimentary environments and potential spatial variations in sediment mobility across the intertidal landscape.

The sandy substrate exhibits a relatively narrow grain size distribution, with much of the material concentrated in the fine to medium sand range (\(150\) to \(300\,\mu\text{m}\)). This distribution is consistent with sediment sorting in environments where tidal currents and wave action can transport finer particles away from exposed surfaces and channels. In contrast, the mudflat samples exhibit a broader distribution, with a principal peak near the fine sand and coarse silt boundary (\(\sim80\) to \(100\,\mu\text{m}\)), a fine grained component extending into silts and clays (\(<10\,\mu\text{m}\)), and secondary coarse fractions around \(800\) to \(1000\,\mu\text{m}\) that may include shell fragments and other biogenic material.

These contrasting sediment distributions are consistent with spatial differences in depositional conditions, from relatively energetic sandy environments to more sheltered settings where fine particles can settle. They provide useful environmental context for interpreting potential responses of the coastal landscape to changes in hydrodynamic forcing. However, the sediment measurements are used here to characterise the study sites and should not be interpreted as direct validation of sediment transport or morphological change unless those processes are explicitly represented and evaluated in the model.

17.6 Geomorphology and Environmental Drivers

Urala’s geomorphology is characterised by extensive barrier spits, dynamic creek mouths, interconnected tidal channels and broad landward salt flats. The North Creek and South Creek systems provide important pathways for tidal exchange across the landscape, while their geometry and connectivity influence how water reaches adjacent intertidal surfaces. These features are particularly relevant when assessing the potential hydrodynamic consequences of infrastructure that intersects or constrains existing flow pathways.

Hydrodynamic conditions are influenced by the regional tidal regime, with semidiurnal tides driving regular inundation and currents within the principal creek channels. Tidal currents and extreme events involving wave action contribute to sediment redistribution, while the relative sheltering of adjacent mudflats and mangrove margins favours the retention of finer material. Tropical cyclones and other intense weather events can temporarily increase wave energy and freshwater discharge, potentially altering creek mouths, barrier spits and sediment distribution. The importance of these episodic processes varies spatially and through time.

The region’s hyperarid climate and high evaporative demand further distinguish Urala from more humid coastal environments. Rainfall is highly variable, and major rainfall events may produce short lived freshwater and sediment inputs against a background of prolonged dry conditions. These climatic and hydrodynamic drivers provide the environmental context for interpreting potential habitat responses.

17.7 Water Level Correction Pipeline

Raw pressure measurements from the water level loggers were processed to estimate water surface elevations relative to the Australian Height Datum (mAHD). The processing procedure comprised three stages: barometric compensation, density correction and vertical datum referencing.

17.7.1 Barometric Correction

The water level loggers were not vented and therefore recorded pressure associated with both the water column and atmospheric pressure. Barometric compensation was required to isolate the pressure exerted by the water column. Two dedicated barometric pressure loggers were deployed, one at Giralia and one at Urala, as shown in Figure 17.15.

The Giralia barometric record did not cover the entire monitoring period. Missing periods were therefore estimated using the relationship between the Giralia and Urala barometric records, as illustrated in Figure 17.16. The resulting barometric pressure series was interpolated to 15 minute intervals to match the temporal resolution used in the subsequent correction procedure.

17.7.1.1 Rugged Troll Loggers

Rugged Troll loggers reported pressure (\(P_{\text{logger,RT}}\)) with a constant barometric offset (\(P_{\text{constant offset}}\)) assigned to each instrument. Absolute pressure was calculated using Equation 17.1:

\[ P_{\text{absolute}} = P_{\text{logger,RT}} + P_{\text{constant offset}} \tag{17.1}\]

Water pressure was then calculated by subtracting the measured barometric pressure, as shown in Equation 17.2:

\[ P_{\text{water,RT}} = P_{\text{absolute}} - P_{\text{barometric}} \tag{17.2}\]

An example of the barometric correction procedure for the Rugged Troll logger at G-MID-CHANNEL is shown in Figure 17.17.

17.7.1.2 AquaTroll Loggers

AquaTroll loggers (\(P_{\text{logger,AT}}\)) did not include the same constant barometric offset as the Rugged Troll instruments. Their water pressure was therefore estimated using the deviation of the measured barometric pressure from its average value, as shown in Equation 17.3:

\[ P_{\text{water,AT}} = P_{\text{logger,AT}} + \left( P_{\text{barometric}} - P_{\text{barometric average}} \right) \tag{17.3}\]

This approach assumes that the logger pressure reading is referenced to approximately the average atmospheric pressure over the relevant period. An example of the correction procedure for the AquaTroll logger at G-MOUTH-CHANNEL-AT is shown in Figure 17.18.

17.7.2 Density Correction

The barometrically corrected pressure measurements were converted to water depth using an estimate of water density. Density was calculated at 15 minute intervals for each monitoring location using Equation 17.4:

\[ \rho = 1000 + 0.75S - 0.2(T - 4) \tag{17.4}\]

where \(\rho\) is water density in kg/m\(^3\), \(S\) is salinity in practical salinity units (PSU), and \(T\) is water temperature in degrees Celsius. Salinity and temperature measurements were interpolated to 15 minute intervals. Temperature was recorded at all monitoring locations throughout the field campaign, whereas salinity observations were not continuously available at every site. A hierarchical fallback procedure was therefore applied to estimate missing salinity values. The procedure prioritised the average salinity recorded at the same site ID, followed by the designated fallback site, the same site location and, finally, the average salinity across the corresponding study area.

The fallback site assignments used in the density calculations are listed in Table 17.2.

The distribution of salinity data sources resulting from this procedure is shown in Figure 17.19. Density estimates were compared with direct density measurements at three monitoring locations. The comparison showed a strong relationship between measured and calculated density, with coefficients of determination exceeding \(0.94\) (Figure 17.20). The calculated density time series for all monitoring locations are presented in Figure 17.21.

Once water density had been estimated, corrected water depth was calculated using Equation 17.5:

\[ H_{\text{corrected}} = \frac{P_{\text{corrected}} \times 10.1972}{\rho/1000} \tag{17.5}\]

where \(H_{\text{corrected}}\) is water depth in metres, \(P_{\text{corrected}}\) is pressure in bar and \(\rho/1000\) is the dimensionless relative water density. The comparison between corrected pressure and calculated depth showed a strong linear relationship, with a correlation coefficient of \(r = 0.996\) (Figure 17.22).

The corrected water depth is compared with the depth reported by each logger in Figure 17.23. The residual distribution is shown in Figure 17.24. The standard deviation of the residuals was \(7.3\,\text{cm}\), which is substantial in the context of the relatively low relief of the intertidal landscape.

17.7.3 Datum Correction

After correcting the pressure measurements for atmospheric pressure and water density, the logger elevations were adjusted to reference the resulting water levels to the Australian Height Datum. The loggers were not individually surveyed, so LiDAR derived ground elevations were used as initial estimates of the elevation at each monitoring location.

Exmouth tide gauge observations, provided in mAHD, were used as an external reference for the datum adjustment. The elevations of the loggers located near the Giralia and Urala channel mouths were adjusted to align the observed spring tide peaks with the corresponding Exmouth tide gauge levels. The calibration procedure is illustrated in Figure 17.25 and Figure 17.26.

Following the mouth logger adjustments, elevations at the remaining monitoring locations were adjusted according to their relative hydraulic connectivity along the tidal flow paths. At Giralia, the calibration proceeded from the mouth channel to the mid channel, mid creek, upper channel and upper mat surface water locations. A similar approach was applied at Urala. Comparisons of monthly peak water levels between hydraulically connected monitoring locations are presented in Figure 17.27 and Figure 17.28. The resulting water level time series before and after datum adjustment are shown in Figure 17.29 and Figure 17.30.

17.8 Results and Hydraulic Dynamics

The corrected water level dataset provides a basis for examining spatial and temporal variations in water levels across the Giralia and Urala estuarine systems. The monitoring records capture variations associated with regular tidal cycles as well as short duration responses to meteorological events. The response to the storm event on 11 April 2024 illustrates the spatial differences in water levels and hydraulic gradients across the intertidal platforms (Figure 17.31 and Figure 17.32).

17.8.1 Spatial Cross Sections and Groundwater Dynamics

Cross sectional profiles provide an overview of spatial and temporal variations in surface water and groundwater elevations from the principal tidal channels towards the upper intertidal areas and cyanobacterial mats (Figure 17.41). Comparisons between paired surface water and groundwater observations also provide information on local hydraulic gradients. These gradients are relevant to understanding potential exchanges between surface water and groundwater, including the movement of saline porewater within the intertidal sediments.

17.9 Application to Hydrodynamic and Eco Morphodynamic Modelling

The processed water level dataset provides an empirical basis for the development and assessment of the Project B hydrodynamic models. The corrected observations were used to characterise baseline hydroperiods and support the calibration and validation of the Delft3D Flexible Mesh hydrodynamic models. The spatially distributed monitoring network also provides information on water level variations and hydraulic gradients across the intertidal landscape.

The paired surface water and groundwater observations provide additional information on local hydraulic conditions and potential subsurface water exchange. These observations are particularly relevant to interpreting the hydrological conditions associated with mangrove and cyanobacterial mat distributions. However, the monitoring records alone do not establish the magnitude or direction of subsurface porewater exchange or hypersaline groundwater discharge. Such processes require additional interpretation of the paired observations and, where appropriate, explicit representation in the modelling framework.
