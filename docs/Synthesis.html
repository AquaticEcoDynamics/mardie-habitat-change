16  Synthesis and Integration

16.1 Overarching Objectives

This research programme addresses a critical knowledge gap in coastal geomorphology and ecology: understanding how hyper arid intertidal ecosystems, particularly mangrove forests and cyanobacterial mats, respond to accelerating sea level rise (SLR) and coastal development. Previous coastal wetland research has predominantly focused on humid tropical mangroves, temperate marshes and sediment rich river deltas, while the dynamics of arid, macrotidal and episodically inundated systems such as those along the West Pilbara coast remain less well understood. By integrating field observations, high resolution spatial datasets and a dynamically coupled biohydromorphodynamic modelling framework, this study evaluated the long term resilience, vulnerability and spatial redistribution of intertidal ecosystems across three distinct study domains: Giralia Bay, Urala and Cape Preston. The modelling framework combines Delft3D Flexible Mesh hydrodynamics with ecological modules and data driven habitat classification approaches. This synthesis brings together the principal findings to establish an integrated understanding of coastal ecosystem responses under low, intermediate and high greenhouse gas emission pathways.

16.2 Methodological Advancements and Paradigm Comparisons

Projecting coastal wetland trajectories over decadal to centennial timescales requires modelling frameworks capable of representing interactions between hydrodynamic forcing, sediment dynamics, morphological evolution and biological processes. Traditional assessments of SLR impacts have often relied on static bathtub models or simplified approaches in which hydrodynamic, morphological and ecological processes are represented separately. Bathtub models estimate inundation from water levels and static topography, without explicitly representing sediment transport, morphological adjustment or biological adaptation. Consequently, they may misrepresent future mangrove migration pathways where steep topography, sediment limitations or other physical constraints restrict landward expansion. Conversely, such approaches cannot adequately represent the potential for vegetation to modify flow resistance, influence sediment deposition and contribute to changes in bed elevation. Similarly, sequentially coupled or offline models may not fully capture the feedbacks between hydrodynamics, morphology and vegetation when these processes are updated independently or at relatively coarse time intervals.

To address these limitations, this study developed and applied a coupled biohydromorphodynamic modelling framework in which hydrodynamic conditions and vegetation related flow resistance interact dynamically. This approach provides a process based representation of the coevolution of intertidal landscapes and vegetation across spatial scales of several kilometres. The framework was complemented by two approaches for representing cyanobacterial mat distributions: Random Forest machine learning classification and threshold based optimisation using differential evolution. These methods account for the distinct characteristics of cyanobacterial mats, whose distribution and persistence are strongly influenced by inundation, hydration and desiccation conditions rather than the slower growth and establishment processes characteristic of woody vegetation. Together, these modelling approaches provide complementary means of evaluating potential habitat suitability, spatial continuity and fragmentation across heterogeneous arid coastal environments.

16.3 Ecosystem Trajectories Across Sea Level Rise Scenarios

The multi decadal projections across the three Shared Socioeconomic Pathway scenarios reveal substantial differences in the predicted responses of West Pilbara intertidal ecosystems to SLR. Under the low emissions pathway SSP119, representing approximately 0.5 m of SLR by 2125 in the scenarios considered, mangrove forests and cyanobacterial mats generally exhibit greater persistence and more gradual spatial redistribution. At Giralia Bay and Urala, intertidal zonation remains comparatively stable over the 100 year projection period, although localised landward migration occurs as rising water levels increase inundation across previously higher elevation salt flats. Cyanobacterial mats also redistribute along and around tidal creek networks, with their persistence and spatial continuity influenced by local topography, inundation conditions and the availability of suitable substrate.

Under the intermediate and high emissions pathways, SSP370 and SSP585, the projected ecological responses become increasingly pronounced. These scenarios represent approximately 0.95 m and 1.25 m of SLR, respectively, by 2125. Increasing water levels initially create additional inundated areas and potential opportunities for landward expansion. At Urala, for example, increased tidal penetration under elevated sea levels promotes substantial headward extension of tidal creeks, with modelled incision extending more than five kilometres inland. Such changes may temporarily facilitate the redistribution of mangroves and cyanobacterial mats by connecting previously less frequently inundated areas to the tidal network. However, this initial expansion does not necessarily translate into long term persistence. Where rates of SLR exceed the capacity of vegetation to track suitable elevations, or where suitable landward areas become unavailable, the modelled distributions become increasingly restricted. Under SSP585, the projections indicate substantial losses of existing mangrove and cyanobacterial mat areas by Year 100, with more than 85% of the original mapped extent becoming submerged in the unconstrained settings considered. Remaining areas become concentrated along elevated basin margins and other locations where local elevation and inundation conditions continue to support persistence. These patterns highlight the potential for substantial coastal squeeze and ecosystem reorganisation under high SLR.

Regional geomorphology and tidal regime further influence the differences in projected responses among the three study sites. Giralia Bay and Urala contain extensive, low gradient upper intertidal flats, where relatively small vertical changes in water level can translate into substantial horizontal shifts in inundation boundaries. These topographic characteristics can facilitate landward redistribution but may also expose large areas to changing inundation regimes as sea levels rise. Cape Preston, by comparison, experiences a larger macrotidal range of approximately 1.5 to 4.2 m and has a more restricted high intertidal area. The SLR increments therefore represent a smaller proportion of its overall tidal range, potentially contributing to the comparatively consistent landward migration patterns predicted for mangroves across the scenarios. Nevertheless, the magnitude of the tidal range alone does not determine ecological sensitivity. Local elevation distributions, inundation frequency, substrate conditions and the availability of suitable land remain important controls on mangrove establishment and persistence. The projected redistribution of cyanobacterial mats at Cape Preston also varies between modelling approaches, demonstrating that the representation of ecological suitability can influence the spatial patterns and extent of future mat distributions.

Coastal development introduces an additional constraint on these natural redistribution processes. At Urala, the implementation of rigid, impermeable salt bund walls restricts the landward migration of mangroves under intermediate and high SLR scenarios. In the unconstrained simulations, the modelled mangrove extent increases to approximately 2.0 to 2.35 times the baseline extent by Year 100. In contrast, development constrained simulations produce substantial canopy retreat, resulting in a net reduction of approximately 32% to 41% in late century mangrove extent relative to the baseline. The bund walls also restrict the development of secondary tidal creek branches, potentially reducing the connectivity between tidal channels and landward areas. These results demonstrate how coastal infrastructure can alter hydrodynamic pathways and limit the capacity of intertidal ecosystems to respond to rising sea levels, even where suitable elevations may otherwise be available.

16.4 Key Assumptions and Model Limitations

Although the modelling framework provides process based insights into arid intertidal ecosystem dynamics, several assumptions and limitations should be considered when interpreting the projections. First, spatial and temporal variations in surface salinity and subsurface porewater dynamics were not fully represented in the primary dynamic vegetation framework. In hyper arid coastal environments, root zone salinity can exert a strong physiological influence on mangrove establishment, growth and survival. Preliminary implementation of a proxy salinity mass balance approach reduced spatial overprediction in some areas, including Urala. However, numerical instabilities associated with wetting and drying under extreme hypersaline conditions, on the order of 100 parts per thousand, limited the implementation of explicit salinity stress feedbacks across the modelling domains. Consequently, the predicted suitable areas primarily reflect physical inundation conditions and specified ecological criteria, rather than a comprehensive assessment of physiological tolerance to hypersaline sabkha soils. In particular, the model may overestimate the potential for landward mangrove migration where saline groundwater, subsurface brine accumulation or restricted tidal flushing creates additional barriers to establishment.

Second, the hydrodynamic simulations were driven by tidal forcing without explicitly incorporating wind waves, storm surges or cyclone related meteorological events. The projections therefore do not represent wave induced erosion along vegetation margins, extreme bed shear stresses or the disturbance and recovery processes associated with major storm events. These processes may substantially influence sediment redistribution, channel evolution and vegetation persistence in exposed coastal environments.

Third, the hydrodynamic models were implemented in two dimensional, depth averaged mode. Although this approach is computationally practical for regional domains spanning hundreds of square kilometres, it does not explicitly resolve vertical stratification or three dimensional circulation processes within deeper tidal channels. These processes may affect local sediment transport, mixing and the distribution of suitable ecological conditions.

Fourth, vegetation colonisation is governed primarily by local environmental suitability criteria and the assumed availability of propagules. Explicit propagule transport, dispersal times, seed viability and establishment probabilities are not fully represented. As a result, the model may predict colonisation in areas that are physically suitable but not necessarily accessible to propagules within the simulated timeframe. Similarly, the ecological modules do not fully represent all processes governing vegetation mortality, recovery and resprouting.

Finally, the representation of sediment accretion is focused on inorganic surface sediment processes, without explicitly resolving organic matter accumulation through peat formation, root production and turnover, or subsurface subsidence. These processes can influence net surface elevation change and the capacity of intertidal ecosystems to maintain their position relative to rising sea levels. Their omission introduces uncertainty into long term projections, particularly in areas where sediment supply is limited or organic matter accumulation makes a substantial contribution to surface elevation.

Taken together, these limitations indicate that the projections should be interpreted as conditional assessments of ecosystem responses under specified physical and ecological assumptions, rather than deterministic forecasts. The results are most informative when used to compare relative responses among SLR scenarios, study sites and development configurations, while recognising that additional physical and biological processes may alter the timing and extent of future changes.

16.5 Management Implications and Adaptive Pathways

The findings provide a basis for informing coastal planning, environmental assessment and infrastructure development along the Pilbara coastline. The potential for substantial mangrove and cyanobacterial mat redistribution under intermediate and high SLR scenarios highlights the importance of maintaining landward accommodation space. Coastal management should therefore consider protecting supratidal buffer areas and migration corridors, particularly where low gradient terrain provides opportunities for ecosystems to shift inland as inundation regimes change. Future infrastructure, including evaporation ponds, access roads and pastoral fencing, should be assessed in relation to projected tidal creek expansion and potential ecosystem migration pathways. Where the model identifies critical landward areas, avoiding or minimising new barriers to tidal connectivity may help retain opportunities for ecological adjustment.

Management assessments should also distinguish between changes in total mapped extent and changes in ecological condition. An apparent persistence or expansion of mangrove area does not necessarily indicate that ecosystem structure and function are being maintained. Under intermediate and high SLR, mangroves may become concentrated along narrow margins or newly incised channels, resulting in fragmented distributions and reduced spatial continuity. Monitoring should therefore consider not only vegetation extent but also canopy density, biomass, fragmentation, connectivity and changes in the elevation and inundation conditions of occupied areas. Similarly, cyanobacterial mats should be recognised as ecologically important primary producers within arid intertidal systems, and their changing distribution and spatial continuity should be incorporated into assessments of ecosystem response.

The substantial scenario divergence projected beyond Year 50 further supports the use of adaptive management approaches. Dynamic Adaptive Policy Pathways provide a useful framework for linking management decisions to observed environmental changes rather than relying solely on fixed planning horizons. Potential triggers could include measured rates of local sea level rise, changes in tidal creek extent, progressive loss of suitable elevation zones, declining mangrove continuity and increasing restrictions on landward migration. Regular monitoring and periodic model reassessment would allow these triggers to be updated as new observations become available. This approach would also help identify where additional field measurements, improved salinity characterisation or more detailed hydrodynamic modelling are required to reduce uncertainty.

Overall, this research demonstrates that the future of arid intertidal ecosystems along the West Pilbara coast will depend on the interaction between sea level rise, tidal hydrodynamics, local geomorphology, ecological processes and coastal development. The coupled modelling framework provides a means of examining these interactions and identifying areas where ecosystem redistribution or loss may be most pronounced. Its application across Giralia Bay, Urala and Cape Preston also demonstrates that responses cannot be inferred from sea level rise magnitude alone, because local topography, tidal regimes and development constraints produce distinct spatial outcomes. Incorporating these site specific controls into coastal planning, while addressing the identified model limitations through targeted monitoring and further model development, will be important for improving assessments of long term coastal ecosystem resilience and informing adaptive management across the Pilbara region.
