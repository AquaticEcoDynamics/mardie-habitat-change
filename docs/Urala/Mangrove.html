11  Mangrove Response to Sea Level Rise

11.1 Sea Level Rise Response

In this section, we present the response of mangroves and creek morphology to different SLR scenarios for the Urala study site. Similar to the Giralia Bay simulations, we evaluated three distinct IPCC Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) SLR scenarios (refer to Section 3.4 for more details) representing low, medium and high SLR scenarios and consider over a 100-year projection period.

The predicted mangrove extent under present-day environmental conditions (Figure 10.3) was used as the initial condition for all runs. The simulations were executed to assess long-term mangrove migration patterns, dieback, and the co-evolution of tidal creek channels under changing hydrodynamics.

Figure 11.1 illustrates the spatial co-evolution of mangrove canopy height (\(h\)) and bed level elevation (\(z\)) across the Urala domain under the three SLR scenarios over the 100-year simulation period.

11.1.1 Mangrove Dynamics and Morphological Evolution

The simulations reveal a strong coupling between sea level rise, intertidal wetland encroachment, and tidal creek network extension across the Urala coastal plain:

  1. Low-SLR Scenario (SSP119): Under SSP119, increases in the tidal prism remain modest because the rate of sea-level rise is relatively low. As a result, the main tidal creeks in SSP119 show minimal expansion or deep headward incision. The existing network geometry accommodates the minor increase in tidal volume without inducing substantial bed erosion or channel widening. Mangroves along the creek banks and within the main basin maintain dynamic equilibrium, experiencing subtle landward growth while keeping pace with water level changes without major dieback.

  2. Moderate to High-SLR Scenarios (SSP370 & SSP585): As sea level rise accelerates in SSP370 and SSP585, larger volumes of water flood the expansive, low-lying supratidal flats behind the main coastal barrier during each tidal cycle. This dramatic increase in the tidal prism drives higher flow velocities within the main drainage arteries, initiating severe scour and progressive channel expansion.

    • Creek Evolution: The main tidal channels migrate landward, widening and deepening into the upper intertidal basin to handle the increased flood/ebb volumes.
    • Mangrove Migration & Dieback: Concurrently, higher baseline water levels drown out the lowermost seaward fringe of mangroves due to prolonged hydroperiod inundation. However, the newly formed tidal channels and headward creek extensions deliver tidal flushing deeper inland, creating favorable hydrodynamics that allow mangroves to colonise new landward accommodation space.
NoteKey Findings: Urala SLR Response Dynamics
  • SSP119 (+0.5 m): Mangrove extents remain stable, and tidal creeks show negligible expansion due to minimal change in the overall tidal prism.
  • SSP370 (+0.9 m): Moderate seaward fringe drown-out, compensated by landward channel extension and inland mangrove encroachment.
  • SSP585 (+1.25 m): Pronounced morphological restructuring. Enhanced tidal prism drives significant tidal creek widening and inland incision, accompanied by broad landward shift and redistribution of mangrove canopy height (\(h\)).

Figure 11.2 shows the spatial distributions of mangroves and tidal channel evolution for Year 50 and Year 100 across the Urala domain, highlighting the diverging long-term trajectory of mangrove extent and bed morphodynamics across SSP119, SSP370, and SSP585 scenarios. Similar to the Giralia site, during the initial ~40–50 years of simulation across all climate projections, mangrove distributions remain relatively comparable, maintaining established populations along the lower intertidal margins and tidal creek networks.

Spatially, baseline mangrove distribution is heavily governed by tidal inundation and bed elevation. However, progressive SLR drives significant channel morphological changes alongside a distinct landward transgression and localised mangrove dieback:

  • SSP119 (Low Emission): At Year 50 (Figure 11.2 a), mangroves remain well-established throughout the central and southern intertidal flats with tall canopy structures (\(h > 2\text{ m}\)). By Year 100 (Figure 11.2 d), mangrove coverage undergoes localised thinning and slight dieback along the middle channels, while lower-canopy mangroves (\(h \approx 1\text{–}1.5\text{ m}\)) establish along the landward boundaries. Creek structures in SSP119 undergo minor expansion, preserving the overall channel geometry.
  • SSP370 (Intermediate Emission): By Year 50 (Figure 11.2 b), slight fragmentation and colonization are visible along the expanding creek channels. By Year 100 (Figure 11.2 e), tidal creeks evolve dramatically, deepening and incision-carving wide, branching channel networks deep into the landward supratidal flats. Mangroves dynamically track this channel incision, establishing dense, tall stands (\(h > 2\text{ m}\)) along the newly formed creek banks and migrating significantly inland into newly inundated tidal zones.
  • SSP585 (High Emission): At Year 50 (Figure 11.2 c), channel enlargement and mangrove establishment mirror the intermediate scenario. By Year 100 (Figure 11.2 f), extreme SLR leads to severe tidal creek incision, mouth widening, and extensive channel network propagation far landward. Mangroves colonise these newly formed, deeply incised creek margins and landward margins; however, rapid sea-level rise causes significant drowning and dieback of lower intertidal mangroves, limiting total net spatial coverage compared to SSP370.
NoteKey Findings: Tidal Creek Incision and Mangrove Relocation

Overall, spatial modelling for the Urala domain demonstrates that under moderate-to-high SLR (SSP370 and SSP585), dramatic tidal creek network expansion and incision serve as the primary pathways for landward mangrove migration.

While deeper, incised channels facilitate significant inland colonization along creek banks, extreme SLR under SSP585 outpaces lower-intertidal bed accretion, leading to seaward drowning and a restricted total habitat footprint.

11.1.2 Morphological Response

To isolate and track the morphological response of the tidal network to sea-level rise (SLR), the active creek network across the Urala domain was extracted using the \(0.2\text{ m}\) bed elevation contour at 25-year intervals (Years 1, 25, 50, 75, and 100) in Figure 11.3. Driven by expanding tidal prism volumes under rising sea levels, severe channel scour, bed incision, and headward erosion push the primary tidal network landward into upper intertidal and supratidal flats over time.

The magnitude and planform complexity of headward channel propagation vary across the climate projections:

  1. SSP119: Channel headward expansion is minor or near-trivial compared to moderate and extreme scenarios. Morphological alterations remain largely confined to minor widening of primary channels, with negligible headward extension into inland flats by Year 100.
  2. SSP370: Driven by accelerated SLR, the creek network undergoes extensive landward extension. Primary and secondary tidal channels carve deep branching networks into the landward flats, extending \(>5\text{ km}\) inland along main tributaries by Year 100.
  3. SSP585: Demonstrates a remarkably similar spatial response and planform network footprint to SSP370, showing only marginal differences in network geometry and reach. Under both moderate (SSP370) and extreme (SSP585) forcings, headward incision reaches a plateau, suggesting that channel extension in the Urala domain becomes topographically constrained once tidal networks fully incise through the inland salt flats.

To evaluate the integrated hydro-morphodynamic and ecological trajectories of the Urala domain under SLR, the normalised time series of total mangrove surface area and tidal creek planform footprint relative to initial conditions were tracked (Figure 11.4).

During the initial 10-15 years of simulation, both mangrove extent and channel planform footprint exhibit relative stability, maintaining normalised values near baseline (\(1.0\)). Early rate-of-rise metrics remain within threshold tolerances for local bed shear stress, allowing localised bed accretion and canopy retention to buffer hydrodynamic shifts without broad structural reorganisation across SSP119, SSP370, and SSP585.

Beyond Year 50, non-linear hydro-morphodynamic bifurcation occurs across scenarios, driving coupled channel expansion (Figure 11.4 b) and ecological redistribution (Figure 11.4 a):

  • Creek Expansion under the Higher-Emissions SLR Scenarios (SSP370 and SSP585): Both SSP370 and SSP585 exhibit rapid, quasi-parallel acceleration in creek planform area past Year 50. Increasing tidal prism volume amplifies ebb-jet velocities which could be triggering widespread headward headcut erosion and lateral secondary branching. While SSP585 drives slightly greater initial incision depth, total planform network footprint expansion converges between SSP370 and SSP585 by Year 100, contrasting sharply with the stable, baseline-constrained trajectory of SSP119.
  • Eco-Morphodynamic Lag and Opportunistic Relocation: Normalised mangrove area highlights a strong eco-geomorphic coupling with channel network evolution. Under SSP370 and SSP585, early landward extension of incised creeks provides newly inundated, sheltered drainage corridors that facilitate a temporal surge in canopy colonisation between Years 50 and 80.
  • Seaward Mangrove Loss and Landward Migration: Under SSP585, increasing sea levels initially promote landward mangrove expansion as newly inundated areas become suitable for colonisation. This expansion is predicted to continue for approximately 50–60 years, resulting in an increase in total mangrove extent. However, this trend subsequently reverses as environmental conditions across parts of the model domain extend beyond the prescribed ranges for mangrove establishment and growth. Once these conditions exceed the modelled tolerance thresholds, the mortality rules are activated, resulting in vegetation loss and a decline in total mangrove area. Consequently, mangrove extent decreases after its initial peak, with greater losses under SSP585 than SSP370 by Year 100. This trajectory reflects the balance between initial landward establishment and subsequent mortality under increasingly unsuitable environmental conditions.

11.1.3 Total Biomass Dynamics Across SLR Scenarios

To quantify systemic ecological productivity under changing climate projections, total domain-wide mangrove biomass was calculated and normalised relative to initial baseline conditions (\(t = 0\)) over the 100-year simulation period (Figure 11.5).

Across all SLR scenarios, total normalised biomass exhibits an initial lag phase during the first 20–30 years, remaining close to baseline values (\(1.0\)). Following this initial lag, significant non-linear divergences occur:

  • SSP119 (Low Emission): Total biomass experiences a gentle expansion, peaking at approximately \(1.25\times\) baseline levels around Year 55–60. As sea-level rise rates stabilise, biomass gradually declines, returning close to initial levels (\(\approx 1.0\)) by Year 100.
  • SSP370 (Intermediate Emission): Biomass surges rapidly past Year 30, reaching a peak of approximately \(1.75\times\) initial biomass between Years 60 and 65. This rapid growth is driven by landward expansion along newly incised creek networks. However, after Year 65, increased submergence triggers a steep decline, settling closer to the baseline by Year 100.
  • SSP585 (High Emission): Total biomass tracks closely with SSP370 during the expansion phase, reaching a secondary peak of \(\approx 1.6\times\) baseline around Year 55–60. Beyond Year 60, extreme inundation outpaces accretion, initiating a sharp biomass contraction down to baseline levels (\(\approx 1.0\)) by Year 100.

11.1.4 SLR Implications on the Initial Mangrove Footprint

To isolate the vulnerability of established forests from opportunistic landward colonisation, the temporal decay of the initial mangrove footprint area and its corresponding biomass dynamics were tracked exclusively within the original baseline boundaries (Figure 11.6). Mangrove footprint area was quantified in hectares, while biomass was normalised relative to its initial value at timestep \(t = 0\).

11.1.4.1 Footprint Area Decay Dynamics

As shown in Figure 11.6 a, the initial mangrove footprint area (\(\approx 2200\text{ ha}\)) experiences progressive, stepwise losses across all scenarios as sea levels rise:

  • Lag Phase (Years 0–30): All scenarios retain approximately \(1950\)–\(2000\text{ ha}\) of their initial footprint, equivalent to around \(89\)–\(91\%\) of the baseline area. During this period, losses are relatively limited and occur primarily through localised reductions in the existing mangrove footprint.

  • Accelerated Loss (Years 30–70): Under SSP370 and SSP585, mangrove footprint losses become increasingly pronounced, consistent with greater exposure to inundation and the associated modelled mortality. By Year 70, the initial footprint has declined to approximately \(1350\text{ ha}\) under SSP370 and \(900\text{ ha}\) under SSP585. The SSP585 trajectory subsequently declines sharply, falling below \(500\text{ ha}\) at approximately Year 72–73.

  • Continued Decline and Scenario Divergence (Years 70–100): Losses continue under SSP370 and SSP585, with both scenarios approaching near-complete depletion of the initial footprint by Year 100. Under SSP585, less than \(100\text{ ha}\) remains, representing less than \(5\%\) of the original area. SSP370 retains approximately \(200\text{ ha}\) (around \(9\%\)), whereas SSP119 retains more than \(1250\text{ ha}\) (approximately \(58\%\)). The SSP119 trajectory therefore indicates a substantially slower decline in the original mangrove footprint compared with the higher sea-level rise scenarios.

11.1.4.2 Normalised Biomass Dynamics within Initial Boundaries

Normalised biomass within the initial footprint (Figure 11.6 b) closely tracks the spatial decay of forest coverage, revealing clear stress mechanisms:

  • Threshold Stress Past Year 35: Biomass remains stable (\(1.0\)) during the first 30 years. Beyond Year 35, hydrodynamic stress (increased hydroperiod and permanent inundation) impairs tree growth and triggers mortality.
  • Divergent Collapse Rates: Past Year 50, normalised biomass in SSP585 decays rapidly, dropping below \(0.1\) (\(10\%\) of initial biomass) by Year 80. SSP370 exhibits a similar steep decline, reaching \(\approx 0.08\) by Year 100. In contrast, SSP119 maintains over \(45\%\) of its initial biomass at Year 100.

11.1.4.3 Drivers of Initial Footprint Loss

Losses within the initial mangrove footprint occur as increasing inundation exceeds the modelled tolerance of established stands, particularly along the lower intertidal margin. This analysis tracks only the original footprint and excludes establishment in new landward areas. It therefore shows the vulnerability of existing stands, rather than demonstrating that landward migration is physically blocked. Persistence of mangroves across the wider domain depends strongly on successful establishment in newly suitable areas along the evolving creek network.

11.1.5 Comparative Transect and Localised Morphodynamics (SSP119 vs. SSP585)

To evaluate the fine-scale eco-geomorphic mechanisms governing mangrove survival and channel realignment, a high-resolution, zoomed-in dynamic comparison was conducted for a critical section within the Urala Creek South domain (Figure 11.7). This detailed focus isolates how opposing SLR extremes (SSP119 vs. SSP585) alter cross-sectional bed profile evolution, creek incision rates, and canopy height distribution over the 100-year simulation.

The bottom cross-sectional transects highlight fundamentally diverging trajectories between low and high emission scenarios:

  • SSP119 Hydro-Geomorphic Stability: Under SSP119 (Figure 11.7 a), tidal forcing remains well within ecological and morphodynamic threshold limits. Bed elevation profile shifts are minor, with localised vertical bed level equilibrium preventing channel drowning. Mangrove canopy heights along the cross-section remain stable, maintaining structural continuity without significant dieback or rapid migration.
  • SSP585 Non-Linear Scour and Landward Shift: Under SSP585 (Figure 11.7 b), accelerated SLR triggers severe bed scour along the primary channel floor, deepening the creek bed while eroding intertidal banks. Concurrently, lower-intertidal mangrove stands undergo progressive canopy collapse due to excessive inundation frequency. However, higher-elevation intertidal margins along the transect experience opportunistic colonisation, where juvenile trees (\(h \approx 1\text{-}1.5\text{ m}\)) establish as tidal boundaries retreat landward.

11.1.6 Influence of Development Envelope

To assess how a physical barrier could affect mangrove migration under sea-level rise, simulations incorporated a hypothetical development footprint based on the original salt-pond proposal. Runs were conducted under the intermediate (SSP370) and high (SSP585) SLR scenarios. In Delft3D Flexible Mesh, the boundary was represented as a rigid, impermeable thin dam wall. This is an illustrative development scenario rather than a representation of an approved development.

The impermeable boundary prevents water and sediment from crossing into the development footprint, restricting tidal inundation and the landward extension of creeks within that area. It therefore provides an explicit physical constraint on potential mangrove migration under sea-level rise. Figure 11.8 shows the study site in Western Australia (Figure 11.8 a) and the boundary used for the hypothetical barrier scenario (Figure 11.8 b).

To systematically evaluate the eco-geomorphic alterations induced by this physical barrier, the comparative analysis focuses on evaluating baseline (unconstrained) conditions against the development scenario under the high-emission forcing (SSP585), representing the most critical stress test for system resilience and spatial adaptation.

For illustrative purposes, we present the spatio-temporal evolution of mangrove height and bed morphodynamics under the SSP585 scenario with the hypothetical development boundary (Figure 11.9). Numerical results indicate that as sea-level rise accelerates, mangroves actively attempt to migrate landward into newly inundated intertidal zones. However, the rigid and impermeable bund walls intercept this natural landward retreat pathway. Mangrove stands initially colonise and cluster along the immediate seaward edges of the bund walls. As sea levels continue to rise throughout the 100-year projection, the physical barrier prevents any further landward migration, forcing the vegetation into prolonged inundation stress, acute coastal squeeze, and eventual canopy dieback.

To further isolate these eco-geomorphic alterations and evaluate the localised impact of the bund walls on mangrove height and channel profile evolution, we systematically compare the zoomed-in transect animations for the SSP585 scenario with and without the development footprint (Figure 11.10).

11.2 Eco-Geomorphic Impacts of Development Bund Walls

To evaluate the influence of coastal development infrastructure, the normalised temporal evolution of mangrove extent and tidal creek footprints under baseline climate scenarios (SSP370 and SSP585) was systematically compared against their development-constrained counterparts over a 100-year simulation period (Figure 11.11). Both metrics are normalised relative to initial conditions at Year 1.

During the initial nine decades (Years 0–90), both baseline and development scenarios exhibit comparable growth patterns, expanding mangrove area up to 3.0–3.3 times the baseline footprint (Figure 11.11 a). Minor variance between years 50 and 75 reflects localised, transient hydro-period adjustments upstream of the bund walls.

Around Years 92-94, the development and no-development trajectories diverge sharply (Figure 11.11 a):

  • Baseline scenarios (SSP370 and SSP585): Mangrove area remains approximately 2.0-2.35 times its initial value by Year 100.
  • Hypothetical development scenarios (DEV_SSP370 and DEV_SSP585): The bund-wall barrier restricts landward migration and mangrove area falls to approximately 1.6 and 1.2 times its initial value, respectively, by Year 100.

This divergence represents a 32% to 41% net reduction in late-century mangrove habitat availability caused directly by the physical bund wall footprint.

11.2.1 Tidal Creek Evolution

The creek-footprint trajectories diverge earlier than the sharp late-century reduction in mangrove area (Figure 11.11 b):

  • Early alignment (Years 0-35): All scenarios follow similar trajectories, with creek footprints increasing by approximately 0.2-0.3%.
  • Divergence (from about Year 35): The hypothetical development scenarios begin to diverge from the baseline trajectories as bund walls restrict landward creek extension and branching.
  • Year 100 comparison: Baseline creek footprints reach approximately 1.019 times the initial value under SSP370 and 1.0205 under SSP585. With the hypothetical bund walls, the corresponding values are approximately 1.014 and 1.015.

The presence of bund walls inhibits natural channel branching, confining hydrodynamics within established pathways and preventing the formation of secondary tidal drainage networks.

NoteSummary: Ecological Trajectories and Infrastructure Impacts at Urala

The simulations show how sea-level rise could change mangrove distribution and tidal creeks at Urala, and how a hypothetical development barrier could restrict the habitats available for landward migration.

11.3 Mangrove and Tidal Creek Responses to Sea-Level Rise

  • SSP119 (Low SLR, 0.5 m): Smaller changes in tidal creeks. The volume of water entering and leaving the intertidal area with each tide changes relatively little, so creek expansion is limited. Some mangroves are lost from the original footprint while others establish in new landward areas. By Year 100, total mangrove area remains above its initial value, while total biomass is close to its initial value.

  • SSP370 (Intermediate SLR, 0.9 m): Landward expansion along tidal creeks. As more water enters and leaves the intertidal area with each tide, creeks extend inland and create areas suitable for mangrove establishment. Mangrove area and biomass initially increase, but some gains are subsequently lost as inundation increases.

  • SSP585 (high SLR, 1.25 m) Extensive losses from the original mangrove footprint. Mangroves establish along newly formed inland creeks, while prolonged inundation causes major losses from existing stands. By Year 100, less than 5% of the original footprint remains. Establishment in new landward areas allows mangroves to persist across the wider domain.

11.4 Effects of the Hypothetical Development Barrier

  • Restricted landward migration: The hypothetical bund walls block water and sediment exchange across the development boundary, limiting creek extension and access to areas where mangroves could otherwise establish as sea level rises.
  • Less mangrove habitat by Year 100: Under SSP370 and SSP585, total mangrove area is approximately 2.0-2.35 times its initial value without the barrier, compared with approximately 1.2-1.6 times its initial value with the hypothetical bund walls.
  • Difference between scenarios: The hypothetical development produces approximately 32-41% less mangrove area by Year 100 than the corresponding scenario without development. Creek expansion and branching are also reduced.

Management implication: Access to suitable landward areas helps mangroves persist as sea level rises in these simulations. The hypothetical bund-wall experiment shows how a barrier can restrict that adaptation and reduce future habitat. These results highlight the need to consider migration pathways when assessing coastal development.
