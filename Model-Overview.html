4  Model Overview


In this study, a state-of-the-art, process-based, open-source Delft3D Flexible Mesh (DFM) model was applied in a depth-averaged (2DH) configuration to simulate the coupled hydrodynamic and morphodynamic processes governing coastal, estuarine, and riverine environments (Deltares, 2021). The model resolves the spatial and temporal evolution of flow, sediment transport, and morphological change using an unstructured grid composed of triangular and quadrilateral elements.

DFM employs a finite volume method to solve the shallow water equations under the hydrostatic pressure assumption and Boussinesq approximation, whereby density variations are only considered in the pressure term. The hydrodynamic core (D-Flow FM) computes water levels, velocities, and salinity, and is dynamically coupled with additional modules including wave dynamics (D-Waves) and sediment transport and morphology (D-Morphology). Interactions with vegetation are implemented via the Basic Model Interface (BMI), allowing feedbacks between hydrodynamics, sediment processes, and ecosystem development.

4.1 Flow Module

Hydrodynamic processes are simulated using the Delft3D-FM flow module, which solves the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations under shallow water assumptions (hydrostatic pressure) and the Boussinesq approximation (Deltares, 2019a).

The three-dimensional momentum equations are:

\[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial u}{\partial z} = -\frac{1}{\rho_0}\frac{\partial p}{\partial x} +\nu\nabla^2u -f_x \tag{4.1}\]

\[ \frac{\partial v}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial v}{\partial z} = -\frac{1}{\rho_0}\frac{\partial p}{\partial y} +\nu\nabla^2v -f_y \tag{4.2}\]

\[ \frac{\partial w}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial w}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial w}{\partial y} + w\frac{\partial w}{\partial z} = -\frac{1}{\rho_0}\frac{\partial p}{\partial z} +\nu\nabla^2w -f_z -\frac{\rho}{\rho_0}g \tag{4.3}\]

where \(u\), \(v\), and \(w\) represent the velocity components in the \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\) directions, respectively. \(p\) is pressure, \(\rho\) and \(\rho_0\) are the fluid and reference densities, \(\nu\) is the kinematic viscosity, \(f_x\), \(f_y\), and \(f_z\) represent Coriolis force components, and \(g\) is gravitational acceleration.

Mass conservation is described by:

\[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial w}{\partial z} =0 \tag{4.4}\]

Under the hydrostatic assumption:

\[ \frac{\partial p}{\partial z} = -\rho g \tag{4.5}\]

For this study, Delft3D-FM is applied using a depth-averaged (2DH) configuration. The governing shallow-water equations are:

Continuity equation

\[ \frac{\partial h}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial(hu)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial(hv)}{\partial y} = 0 \tag{4.6}\]

Momentum equation in the x-direction

\[ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + g\frac{\partial h}{\partial x} = -fv + \frac{1}{\rho_0} \left( \frac{\partial\tau_{xx}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\tau_{xy}}{\partial y} + \frac{\tau_{bx}}{h} \right) \tag{4.7}\]

Momentum equation in the y-direction

\[ \frac{\partial v}{\partial t} + u\frac{\partial v}{\partial x} + v\frac{\partial v}{\partial y} + g\frac{\partial h}{\partial y} = fu + \frac{1}{\rho_0} \left( \frac{\partial\tau_{yx}}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\tau_{yy}}{\partial y} + \frac{\tau_{by}}{h} \right) \tag{4.8}\]

where \(h\) is water depth, \(\tau_{xx}\), \(\tau_{xy}\), \(\tau_{yx}\), and \(\tau_{yy}\) are turbulent stresses, and \(\tau_{bx}\) and \(\tau_{by}\) are bed shear stress components.

Vegetation effects on hydrodynamics are incorporated using the formulation of Baptist et al. (2007), where vegetation-induced drag is represented through an increase in the effective bed roughness. The equivalent Chézy roughness coefficient is calculated as:

\[ C = C_b + \frac{\sqrt{g}}{\kappa} \ln \left( \frac{h}{h_{\mathrm{veg}}} \right) \sqrt{ 1+ \frac{C_D n_b h_{\mathrm{veg}} C_b^2} {2g} } \tag{4.9}\]

The vegetation resistance parameter is calculated as:

\[ \lambda = C_D n_b \frac{h_{\mathrm{veg}}}{h} \frac{C_b^2}{C^2} \tag{4.10}\]

where \(C\) is the effective Chézy roughness coefficient, \(C_b\) is the bare-bed Chézy coefficient, \(\kappa\) is the von Kármán constant, \(h\) is water depth, \(h_{\mathrm{veg}}\) is vegetation height, \(C_D\) is the drag coefficient, and \(n_b\) is vegetation stem density. The parameter \(\lambda\) represents the additional flow resistance generated by vegetation. These formulations allow vegetation effects to be incorporated into the hydrodynamic model by modifying flow resistance and consequently altering bed shear stresses and flow velocities.

4.2 Wave Module

Wave dynamics are simulated using the Delft3D-WAVE module (D-Waves), which solves the evolution of random, short-crested wind-generated waves. In the presence of currents, wave action density is conserved rather than wave energy; therefore, the wave field is described using the spectral action balance equation (Hasselmann et al., 1973; Deltares, 2019c):

\[ \frac{\partial N}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial (c_x N)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial (c_y N)}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial (c_\sigma N)}{\partial \sigma} + \frac{\partial (c_\theta N)}{\partial \theta} = \frac{S}{\sigma} \tag{4.11}\]

where \(N\) is the wave action density, \(c_x\) and \(c_y\) are propagation velocities in geographic space, \(c_\sigma\) represents the propagation velocity in relative frequency space, and \(c_\theta\) represents propagation in directional space. The source term \(S\) includes wave generation, dissipation, and nonlinear wave–wave interactions.

Wave attenuation by vegetation is included as an additional dissipation source term in the wave action balance equation following Méndez and Losada (2004) and Suzuki et al. (2012):

\[ S_{ds,\mathrm{veg}} = -\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} g^2 \widetilde{C_D} b_{\mathrm{veg}} n_b \left( \frac{\tilde{k}}{\tilde{\sigma}} \right) \frac{ \sinh^3(\tilde{k}h_{\mathrm{veg}}) + 3\sinh(\tilde{k}h_{\mathrm{veg}}) } { 3\tilde{k}\cosh^3(\tilde{k}h) } \sqrt{E_{\mathrm{tot}}} E(\sigma,\theta) \tag{4.12}\]

where \(S_{ds,\mathrm{veg}}\) is the vegetation-induced wave dissipation term, \(\widetilde{C_D}\) is the drag coefficient, \(b_{\mathrm{veg}}\) is vegetation stem diameter, \(n_b\) is vegetation stem density, \(\tilde{k}\) is the mean wave number, \(\tilde{\sigma}\) is the mean wave frequency, \(h_{\mathrm{veg}}\) is vegetation height, \(h\) is water depth, \(E_{\mathrm{tot}}\) is total wave energy, and \(E(\sigma,\theta)\) is the wave energy density spectrum over wave frequency and direction.

4.3 Morphodynamic Module

Sediment transport and morphological evolution are simulated using the Delft3D-FM morphodynamic module (D-Morphology). Suspended sediment transport is calculated using the advection–diffusion equation:

\[ \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial (uc)}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial (vc)}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial ((w-w_s)c)}{\partial z} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \left( \epsilon_{s,x} \frac{\partial c}{\partial x} \right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \left( \epsilon_{s,y} \frac{\partial c}{\partial y} \right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \left( \epsilon_{s,z} \frac{\partial c}{\partial z} \right) \tag{4.13}\]

where \(c\) is the sediment concentration, \(u\), \(v\), and \(w\) are the flow velocity components in the \(x\), \(y\), and \(z\) directions, respectively, \(w_s\) is the sediment settling velocity, and \(\epsilon_{s,x}\), \(\epsilon_{s,y}\), and \(\epsilon_{s,z}\) are sediment diffusivities in each spatial direction.

For the present depth-averaged (2DH) model configuration, vertical transport terms are neglected. Boundary conditions at the free surface and bed define the exchange of suspended sediment between the water column and the seabed. At the free surface, the vertical diffusive flux is assumed to be zero:

\[ -w_s c - \varepsilon_{s,z}\frac{\partial c}{\partial z}=0, \qquad \text{at } z=\zeta \; (\text{free surface}) \tag{4.14}\]

The exchange of sediment between the bed and the water column is represented through erosion and deposition fluxes. The bed boundary condition is expressed as:

\[ -w_s c-\varepsilon_{s,z}\frac{\partial c}{\partial z} = D-E, \qquad \text{at } z=z_b \; (\text{bed level}) \tag{4.15}\]

where \(E\) and \(D\) represent the erosion and deposition fluxes, respectively. For cohesive sediments, erosion and deposition are calculated using the Partheniades–Krone formulations (Partheniades, 1965):

\[ E= \begin{cases} M \left( \frac{\tau_{b,\max}}{\tau_{cr,e}}-1 \right), & \text{when } \tau_{b,\max}>\tau_{cr,e}, \\[8pt] 0, & \text{when } \tau_{b,\max}\leq\tau_{cr,e}, \end{cases} \tag{4.16}\]

\[ D= \begin{cases} w_s c \left( 1-\frac{\tau_{b,\max}}{\tau_{cr,d}} \right), & \text{when } \tau_{b,\max}<\tau_{cr,d}, \\[8pt] 0, & \text{when } \tau_{b,\max}\geq\tau_{cr,d}. \end{cases} \tag{4.17}\]

where \(M\) is the erosion parameter, \(\tau_{b,\max}\) is the maximum bed shear stress generated by currents and waves, \(\tau_{cr,e}\) is the critical bed shear stress for erosion, \(\tau_{cr,d}\) is the critical bed shear stress for deposition, \(w_s\) is the sediment settling velocity, and \(c\) is the depth-averaged suspended sediment concentration.


The coupled hydrodynamic, wave, and morphodynamic processes provide the physical framework required to simulate coastal ecosystem development, including feedbacks between vegetation establishment, flow modification, sediment dynamics, and morphological change.

Overall, the Delft3D-FM framework enables an integrated simulation of hydrodynamics, waves, sediment transport, and vegetation feedbacks, providing a robust tool for assessing coastal ecosystem dynamics under varying environmental conditions.

4.4 Mangrove Model

Mangrove establishment, growth, and mortality were explicitly coupled to local hydrodynamics through an inundation suitability framework. Similar to existing studies (Berger & Hildenbrandt, 2000; Van Maanen et al., 2015; Xie et al., 2022), mangrove growth and survival were governed by an inundation suitability function, \(I(P)\), defined as a quadratic response to inundation frequency \(P\):

\[ I(P) = \max \left( a_s P^2 + b_s P + c_s,\, 0 \right) \tag{4.18}\]

where \(a_s = -13.22\), \(b_s = 8.59\), and \(c_s = -0.40\).

This formulation produces a unimodal response with a maximum suitability (\(S \approx 1.0\)) within the ecologically viable inundation range. Suitability is further constrained by a colonization window such that \(I(P) = 0\) for \(P < P_{\min}\) and \(P > P_{\max}\), where \(P_{\min} = 0.05\) and \(P_{\max} = 0.60\). Outside this range, establishment is not permitted, reflecting functional limits on mangrove tolerance to inundation.

4.4.1 Colonization

Colonization is controlled jointly by inundation frequency and hydrodynamic bed shear stress. The bed shear stress threshold for colonization is fixed as:

\[ \tau_{90} < \tau_{\text{thres}} \quad \text{where} \quad \tau_{\text{thres}} = 0.265 \text{ N m}^{-2} \]

The adopted bed shear stress threshold falls within the range of existing literature (Beselly et al., 2023; Willemsen et al., 2022; Xie et al., 2022). Within this feasible domain, the probability of establishment is proportional to \(I(P)\). When shear stress exceeds the threshold (\(\tau_{90} \ge \tau_{\text{thres}}\)), colonization is completely inhibited regardless of inundation suitability. Similarly, outside the inundation window (\(P < P_{\min}\) or \(P > P_{\max}\)), establishment is impossible even under low hydrodynamic stress.

4.4.2 Vegetation Growth

Vegetation growth is modelled as a diameter increment rate \(\frac{dD}{dt}\), scaled by inundation suitability:

\[ \frac{dD}{dt} = \frac{G \cdot D \left( 1 - \frac{D \cdot H}{D_{\max} \cdot H_{\max}} \right)}{274 + 3 b_2 D - 4 b_3 D^2} \cdot I(P) \tag{4.19}\]

where \(G = 4.39\) is a growth coefficient, \(D\) is stem diameter (\(\text{cm}\)), and tree height \(H\) (\(\text{cm}\)) is given by:

\[ H = 137 + b_2 D - b_3 D^2 \]

with coefficients \(b_2 = 21.3\), \(b_3 = 0.2\), \(D_{\max} = 10 \text{ cm}\), and \(H_{\max} = 330 \text{ cm}\). These coefficients were optimized using idealized simulations against LiDAR measurements at Giralia but modified for Urala and Cape Preston.

Growth is maximized at intermediate inundation frequencies where \(I(P)\) peaks, defining an optimal growth zone within the colonization window. As trees approach their maximum size (\(D \to D_{\max}\)), the growth rate asymptotically approaches zero due to structural limitation constraints, resulting in negligible growth for mature individuals.

4.4.3 Intraspecific Competition

To account for resource limitations and spatial constraints as vegetation matures, intraspecific competition was modelled using a sigmoidal density-dependent competition stress factor (\(C_f\)) that confines vegetation density both between and within grid cells. It is calculated as a function of total standing biomass per cell (\(B_{\text{total}}\)):

\[ C_f = \frac{1}{1 + e^{d \cdot (B_{0.5} - B_{\text{total}})}} \tag{4.20}\]

where \(0 \le C_f \le 1\) represents external stressors, \(B_{0.5}\) represents the half-saturation biomass threshold at which competition stress reaches \(50\%\) (\(C_f = 0.5\)), and \(d\) is a scaling parameter governing the steepness of the stress response.

Under low biomass conditions (\(B_{\text{total}} \ll B_{0.5}\)), \(C_f\) approaches zero, imposing negligible competitive limitations. As canopy closure and root crowding progress (\(B_{\text{total}} \gg B_{0.5}\)), \(C_f\) asymptotically approaches unity, reflecting maximum competition (acting as a proxy for light, space, and porewater nutrients). This competition factor directly modulates individual tree growth rates and exacerbates baseline environmental stress in densely populated grid cells.

4.4.4 Mortality and Self-Thinning

Vegetation mortality and density regulation were executed as a multi-factor, two-stage process influenced by hydrodynamic stress, density-dependent competition, and desiccation. Total environmental stress was computed as the joint product of inundation suitability (\(I(P)\)) and competition stress (\(C_f\)). Sub-optimal conditions were identified wherever total stress fell below a critical threshold:

\[ I(P) \times C_f \le 0.45 \]

For all grid cells outside suitable conditions, a persistent stress counter (\(\text{Mort}\)) was incremented sequentially; otherwise, the counter was reset to zero.

  1. Complete Mortality: Triggered when a cell experiences sustained stress for five consecutive time steps (\(\text{Mort} \ge 5\), equivalent to 5 morphodynamic years) while simultaneously remaining below the critical threshold (\(I(P) \times C_f \le 0.45\)). Upon complete mortality, all structural vegetation attributes—including stem density, stem diameter, canopy height, and root density—were reset to zero.
  2. Self-Thinning: In grid cells subjected to stress (\(I(P) \times C_f \le 0.45\)) that did not fulfill the complete mortality duration criteria, self-thinning was applied by incrementally reducing plant stem density by a fixed mortality rate factor (\(\text{Mort}_{\text{plant}}\)), after which \(C_f\) was dynamically re-evaluated to reflect the updated biomass density.

Similar to existing work (Xie et al., 2022), we assume a mature mangrove can have at most 1,000 roots. The number of roots is calculated following the approach of Van Maanen et al. (2015).
